# Blood Donor Eligibility & Knowledge Base (informational, not medical advice)

This is an editable knowledge base used by the eligibility chatbot. It draws
on common guidance published by the WHO, the American Red Cross, the National
Blood Transfusion Service of India, and standard hematology references. Always
defer to the medical staff at the blood bank for final eligibility decisions.

---

## Age and weight

- Minimum age for whole-blood donation is 18 years. Some countries allow 17 with parental consent.
- Maximum age for first-time donors is typically 60 years; repeat donors can often donate until 65.
- Minimum body weight is 50 kilograms (110 pounds).
- Donors must be in generally good health on the day of donation.

## Frequency between donations

- Whole-blood donors must wait at least 56 days (8 weeks) between donations.
- Double red-cell (Power Red) donors must wait at least 112 days (16 weeks).
- Platelet (apheresis) donors can donate as often as every 7 days, up to 24 times per year.
- Plasma-only (plasmapheresis) donors can donate as often as every 28 days.

## Hemoglobin and vitals

- Hemoglobin must be at least 12.5 g/dL for women and 13.0 g/dL for men.
- Blood pressure must be between 90/50 mmHg and 180/100 mmHg at the time of donation.
- Pulse must be between 50 and 100 beats per minute (athletes with lower resting pulse may still qualify).
- Body temperature must be 37.5 C (99.5 F) or less.

## Recent illness, medication, and procedures

- Defer donation for at least 14 days after recovery from a cold, flu, or fever.
- After taking antibiotics for a bacterial infection, defer for 7 days after the final dose.
- After a COVID-19 infection, defer for at least 14 days after symptoms resolve.
- Most common medications (blood pressure, statins, allergy meds, oral contraceptives, hormone replacement) do NOT defer donation.
- Aspirin and ibuprofen do NOT defer whole-blood donation but do defer platelet donation by 48 hours.
- Isotretinoin (Accutane) requires a 1-month deferral after the last dose.
- Finasteride / dutasteride / hair-loss medications require a 1-month deferral after the last dose.

## Tattoos, piercings, and acupuncture

- Tattoos done at a state-regulated parlor with sterile single-use equipment: NO deferral in most US states; otherwise defer 3 to 6 months depending on local rules.
- Body piercings with sterile single-use equipment: NO deferral; otherwise defer 3 to 6 months.
- Acupuncture by a licensed practitioner with sterile needles: NO deferral.

## Travel

- After travel to a malaria-endemic country, defer donation for 3 months.
- After living in a malaria-endemic country for more than 5 years, defer for 3 years after departure.
- After travel to a Zika-affected area, defer for 4 weeks.

## Pregnancy and breastfeeding

- Defer donation for 6 weeks after giving birth.
- Pregnant donors are not eligible.
- Breastfeeding mothers may donate if the baby is older than 9 months and they are well-nourished, though many programs prefer to wait until weaning.

## Surgery and dental work

- After major surgery, defer for 6 months and only resume once fully recovered.
- After minor outpatient surgery, defer for 7 days.
- After routine dental cleaning, defer for 24 hours.
- After a tooth extraction or root canal, defer for 72 hours.

## Vaccinations

- Inactivated vaccines (flu shot, COVID-19 mRNA vaccines, tetanus, hepatitis A/B): NO deferral.
- Live-attenuated vaccines (MMR, varicella, yellow fever, nasal flu): defer for 4 weeks.
- Smallpox vaccine: defer for 8 weeks.

## Permanent deferrals (do NOT donate)

- A confirmed positive test for HIV, HTLV, or hepatitis B/C.
- A history of Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, or a family history of variant CJD.
- A history of receiving a dura mater (brain covering) graft.
- A history of injecting non-prescription drugs.
- A history of babesiosis or Chagas disease.

## On the day of donation

- Eat a healthy iron-rich meal in the 3 hours before donation.
- Drink at least 500 mL (about 16 oz) of water in the hour before donating.
- Avoid fatty foods (burgers, fries, ice cream) within 24 hours of donating — high lipids can interfere with blood tests.
- Wear short sleeves or a top with sleeves you can roll up easily.
- Bring a government-issued photo ID, a list of medications, and your donor card if you have one.

## What happens during donation

- Registration and a brief health screening: 10–15 minutes.
- A finger prick to check your hemoglobin level: 1 minute.
- Whole-blood donation itself: about 8–10 minutes for ~450 mL.
- Platelet/plasma apheresis: 1.5 to 2.5 hours, since blood is drawn, processed, and returned in cycles.
- Most people feel completely normal during the draw — a small pinch at the start, then nothing.
- After donation, you'll rest 10–15 minutes with juice and a snack to stabilize blood sugar.
- Total visit for whole blood: usually under an hour.

## After donation: recovery

- Avoid heavy lifting or strenuous exercise for 24 hours after donating.
- Keep the bandage on for at least 4 hours and the donation site dry.
- Drink extra fluids for the next 24 to 48 hours (water, juice, decaf — limit alcohol and caffeine).
- Eat iron-rich meals: red meat, spinach, lentils, beans, fortified cereals.
- If you feel light-headed afterward, sit or lie down with feet elevated until it passes.
- A small bruise at the needle site is normal and clears in a few days.
- The body replaces plasma within 24 hours, platelets within 72 hours, and red cells within 4–6 weeks.

## Iron-rich foods to recover faster

- Animal sources (heme iron, best absorbed): red meat, chicken liver, oysters, sardines, tuna.
- Plant sources (non-heme iron): spinach, kale, lentils, chickpeas, kidney beans, tofu, fortified cereals, pumpkin seeds, dried apricots, raisins.
- Pair non-heme iron with vitamin C (citrus, bell peppers, tomatoes, strawberries) for better absorption.
- Avoid coffee or tea within 1 hour of an iron-rich meal — they reduce iron absorption.

## Blood type compatibility (who can give to whom)

- O negative (O-) is the universal red-cell donor: any patient can safely receive O- red cells.
- AB positive (AB+) is the universal red-cell recipient: people with AB+ can receive red cells from any blood type.
- O+ donors can give red cells to: O+, A+, B+, AB+. They can receive only from O+ and O-.
- O- donors can give red cells to: everyone. They can receive only from O-.
- A+ donors can give red cells to: A+, AB+. They can receive from A+, A-, O+, O-.
- A- donors can give red cells to: A+, A-, AB+, AB-. They can receive from A- and O-.
- B+ donors can give red cells to: B+, AB+. They can receive from B+, B-, O+, O-.
- B- donors can give red cells to: B+, B-, AB+, AB-. They can receive from B- and O-.
- AB+ donors can give red cells to AB+ only — but can receive from anyone.
- AB- donors can give red cells to: AB+, AB-. They can receive from AB-, A-, B-, O-.
- The Rh factor matters: Rh-negative recipients can only safely receive Rh-negative red cells. Rh-positive recipients can receive either.
- Plasma compatibility is the inverse of red-cell compatibility: AB plasma is the universal plasma donor, O plasma can only go to O recipients.
- Platelet compatibility is more flexible than red cells but still respects ABO and Rh in most protocols.

## What donated blood is used for

- Trauma and accident victims often need rapid transfusions of red cells and plasma.
- Surgery patients may receive whole blood or red cells during major procedures.
- Cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy frequently need platelets when their counts drop.
- People with sickle cell disease often require regular transfusions throughout their lives.
- Premature babies sometimes need very small, specially-prepared red-cell transfusions.
- Burn patients need plasma to replace lost fluids and proteins.
- Haemophilia and other clotting disorders are treated with plasma-derived factor concentrates.

## Common myths and facts

- Myth: "Donating makes you weak for weeks." Fact: Most donors feel normal within a day. Avoid heavy exercise for 24 hours, then resume normal activities.
- Myth: "I'll catch HIV from donating." Fact: All needles are sterile, single-use, and disposed of safely. There is zero risk of infection from donating.
- Myth: "I have a tattoo, so I can never donate." Fact: In most US states, tattoos from licensed parlors mean no deferral. In countries with stricter rules, the deferral is typically 3–6 months, then you're eligible again.
- Myth: "Vegetarians/vegans can't donate." Fact: They absolutely can, as long as their hemoglobin meets the threshold. Plant-based donors should focus on iron-rich foods and pair them with vitamin C.
- Myth: "It hurts a lot." Fact: Most donors describe it as a brief pinch, then nothing. The needle is small and the draw is steady.
- Myth: "My blood type is too rare to be useful." Fact: Rare types are MORE valuable, not less. Hospitals actively seek out rare-type donors.

## Pediatric donation

- Children cannot donate blood. Minimum age in most countries is 16 or 18.
- Pediatric patients (children) often receive specially-processed red cells in smaller volumes.
- Cord blood (stem cells from the umbilical cord at birth) can sometimes be donated and used for life-saving transplants.

## Plasma, platelets, and whole-blood — what's the difference?

- Whole blood: a full ~450 mL donation that is later separated into red cells, plasma, and platelets in the lab. Standard donation, 56 days between donations.
- Apheresis platelets: a machine separates platelets from your blood and returns the rest. Takes longer (~2 hours) but you can donate every 7 days.
- Plasmapheresis: similar machine, separates plasma. Takes about 1 hour, can donate every 28 days.
- Power Red (double red): a machine collects two units of red cells. Takes about 25–30 minutes, but you must wait 112 days before donating again.

## What to do in an emergency

- In a real medical emergency, call your local emergency number first (112 in India, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK).
- Donato helps connect you with potential donors but does not replace medical professionals or hospital blood banks.
- Hospitals maintain emergency blood reserves and will arrange compatible blood for transfusion regardless of whether you find a matching donor through this app.
- If you are panicking about a family member's blood need: take a breath, call the hospital, and let them coordinate. The hospital will know exactly how much and what type they need.

## Why donate?

- One whole-blood donation can save up to 3 lives.
- Less than 10% of the eligible population donates blood in most countries.
- The body replaces the plasma lost during donation within 24 hours and the red cells within 4 to 6 weeks.
- Regular donors have slightly lower resting heart rates and may have a small reduction in cardiovascular disease risk, though research is mixed.
- Donating costs nothing, takes about an hour, and is one of the few medical interventions where ordinary people can directly save another human's life.
